## Core Concept
Platinum treats price as a testable estimate, not a calculation: choose a band for your niche, lean toward the low or high end according to how your past offers sold, launch, and adjust with evidence while protecting the goodwill of early members. The revenue goal is plain arithmetic, members multiplied by revenue per member, and the $100k mentors reached it from very different points on that curve (about 600 members at $226/month versus about 7,900 at $39/month). What decides the right point is how many people your traffic can bring to the page, how many of them can comfortably say yes to the number, and how long they stay. Mentors repeat one regret in both directions: moving the price around too much, and drifting out of their audience's budget.

### Framework: Niche Price Band
**When to use:** Choosing the first monthly price, or sanity-checking one you already set.
**Steps/Template:** The lesson lists ranges drawn from top-earning communities in each niche.
| Niche | Monthly range | Midpoint |
|---|---|---|
| Hobbies | $29-55 | $42 |
| Music | $39-75 | $57 |
| Money | $65-200 | $132 |
| Spirituality | $30-70 | $50 |
| Tech | $70-110 | $90 |
| Health | $30-70 | $50 |
| Sports | $50-170 | $110 |
| Self-improvement | $50-120 | $85 |
| Relationships | $35-100 | $67 |
1. Find the niche, take the range, then apply Past-Offer Anchoring (next framework) to land low, middle or high.
2. Treat the number as a starting test, not a verdict. Nick Saraev says a lot of pricing is a guess you put on the table and then raise until people stop paying; if nobody pays, you are too high.

### Framework: Past-Offer Anchoring
**When to use:** Deciding where inside the band to start.
**Steps/Template:**
| Your history | Price at |
|---|---|
| Earlier offers sold well at the low end | low end of the band |
| Earlier offers sold well at the high end | high end |
| No prior offers | middle |
- Nick's approach to first prices: he looked at the market (some groups were about $20, some $100) and chose small, reasoning it is easier to raise than lower; if someone paid ~$46 and you later cut to $40 they ask why they paid more.

### Framework: Platinum Milestone Ladder
**When to use:** Planning how many paid members the Platinum clock requires.
**Steps/Template:** The program's own ladder is MRR growth (new MRR minus churned MRR) counted from the timer start: +$1K by day 30, +$5K by day 60, +$10K by day 90, +$20K by day 120, +$30K by day 150. Miss a milestone and the member is removed. The clock officially starts Nov 1 (setup period before that), with the first milestone deadline Nov 30; launching early lets growth since Oct 1 count.
| Price | +$1K | +$5K | +$10K | +$20K | +$30K |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $25 | 40 | 200 | 400 | 800 | 1,200 |
| $50 | 20 | 100 | 200 | 400 | 600 |
| $100 | 10 | 50 | 100 | 200 | 300 |
| $200 | 5 | 25 | 50 | 100 | 150 |
- The "Big Idea" example: 10 members at $50 is $500 MRR, which many creators would call a flop; averaging 6 new members per day for 150 days gets to about $35K MRR even with churn (gross would be ~900 members x $50 = $45K, so the lesson implies roughly 20% leakage; my derivation).
- The first onboarding video says the ranking is by growth, not audience size: a 52k-follower account out-earned much larger ones in the leaderboard shown.

### Framework: Traffic and Conversion Math
**When to use:** Working backward from a member target to the traffic needed.
**Steps/Template:** `Visitors needed = new members wanted / about-page conversion rate`.
| Observed conversion | Source |
|---|---|
| 2.4% rising to 3.4% after adding a long video-testimonial compilation | James Lu |
| ~3% at best, ~2.1% later | Oscar Patel (he says more page visitors beat optimising percentages; 200,000 visitors "can't lose") |
| 4.9-5.0% (2,878 visitors, 142 signups in 30 days) | Member 452e's dashboard in a wins post |
| 1.8% (5,208 visitors, 92 signups) | Taekwondo group in the past-wins images |
| ~2 subs per 100 visits | a member in "What's your count?" |
| 5% on a pay-only launch | Member f1e9 Bolt (video-only vision pitch) |
- Average $100k+/month community: about 43,000 about-page views a month. At 2% that is ~860 signups; at $50 that is ~$43K of new MRR before churn (my arithmetic).
- Source mix matters: Nick says about 28% of his members come through Skool's own network; the host says the average group gets around 30% from Skool discovery. Nick also found roughly a 20x conversion gap between YouTube and Instagram, matching a 20x gap in average watch time.
- Josh's quantity check: about $140K MRR from fewer than 2,000 customers, so view counts are not the lever; tight niche fit and offer quality are.

### Framework: How MRR Is Counted and Plan-Fee Break-Even
**When to use:** Choosing plans (monthly vs annual vs high-ticket) while chasing the leaderboard, or choosing a Skool subscription plan.
**Steps/Template:**
- Counts toward leaderboard MRR: monthly and annual recurring (annual divided by 12, so $1,200/year adds $100), plus high-ticket recurring tiers (a $50 plan next to a $1,000/month VIP tier both add). Does not count: one-time payments, even if sold inside the classroom.
- Skool plans as shown in the lesson images: Hobby $9/month with 10% transaction fee and no custom URL or affiliates; Pro $99/month with 2.9% fee, custom URL and affiliates.
- Break-even: extra $90 monthly fee / 7.1 points of fee saved ~ $1,270, which the call rounds to about $1,300 MRR; below that, Hobby is cheaper, above it, Pro is. Plan can be changed anytime.

### Framework: Start Low and Ratchet Up
**When to use:** First launch, when nobody can tell you the right number.
**Steps/Template:**
1. Launch at an accessible price inside the band.
2. Raise as value accumulates (new recordings, calls, features), in modest steps.
3. Keep the price stable between steps.
- Nick Saraev's pattern: his first community started around $20/month (one clip's caption reads "20 bucks", the longer video's caption garbles it as "208"); he raised weekly as call recordings accumulated until reaching a ceiling he describes as roughly "500" (the caption does not say dollars or members). Maker School started around $46-47, climbed to $184, with "$194" shown as the pending price.
- His cost: starting very low drew buyers who questioned every dollar; he eventually told some to pay more or leave, and says he would have started near $50-60 for the first community. On Maker School he thinks $184 is above some of his audience's means and he would trade some price for more members and network effect; after raising he found he could not walk it back and instead considers a cheaper or a premium second offer.
- Josh Madakor's pattern: $384/month at launch (unsure of infrastructure cost), cut to $97 once he learned members use about $5 each of cloud, later raised to $99 then $129. He says he bounced around too much and would stay more stable. He also says he would have used $99 rather than $97.
- James Lu's pattern: $99 because the platform then capped group price at $100; "I didn't put any thought into the price."
- Jade's pattern: $500 ebook-plus-community entry, then $500/year, $1,500/year, now three monthly tiers ($50/$100/$200); she says there was no pricing strategy, raised partly to slow traffic and members "still buy", and justifies each increase with value added (daily live trading, Tuesday sessions, more modules).

### Framework: Price-Increase Mechanics
**When to use:** Raising price on a live group.
**Steps/Template:**
- Existing members keep the rate they joined at (lesson note), so each raise adds a new tier of payers.
- Trigger by member count or by date. Nick: "price rises when we hit [member count]"; he notes join rate speeds up as the threshold nears, unlike date-based clocks. In a YouTube CTA he says he raises the price every 100 members.
- Show the current price in the sidebar so a member thinking of leaving knows a return would cost the full price (a churn reducer he lists).
- Expect spikes and noisy analytics: Josh's member chart jumps came from announced increases (he also changed the yearly price at the same time); people rush in, and later churn from that cohort muddies reporting.
- Tie increases to added value and keep them gradual and fair (Nick); do not change price or lock content phases every other week (Oscar says people adapt to the baseline and goodwill is damaged).
- Cutting is harder than raising; a lower price offends earlier payers.
**Example from source:** classroom/start-here/15-...md (lock-in note); 01-nick-saraev-q-a-250k-mo.md [~0:51:46]; transcripts/start-here/28-youtube-launch-posts-ref-jBF48jNWPJE.md [~0:22:20]; transcripts/start-here/02-2-100k-month-examples.md [0:45:14]; 04-josh-madakor-q-a-130k-mo.md [~0:14:17, ~0:33:24]; 02-oscar-patel-q-a-6-figure-mo.md [~0:56:53].

### Framework: Founding-Rate Urgency
**When to use:** Launch week, so early buyers get a reason to act now.
**Steps/Template:**
1. Pick a founding price below your intended ongoing price.
2. Cap it by a real number of spots or a real deadline, and have a real reason (personal feedback, small calls, onboarding capacity).
3. Say the future price in the same sentence.
4. Repeat on every launch surface: tease posts, YouTube end CTA (first ~25 get the founding rate plus a 1:1 call), Instagram story-to-DM automation (the DM states the discounted rate and the date it rises), email sequence (pre-launch, founding offer, what your first week looks like, objections, last call).
5. Honor the deadline.
| Example | Founding offer | Normal price | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Poop Scoop Academy email | $29/month "forever", 29-hour window | $49 | time limit, first-20 prize |
| Mikey No Code email | first 25 members at $29/month | $299/month | seat limit, content live in one week |
| School of Mentors 2nd-anniversary DM | $29/month for 24 hours | $49 | ManyChat keyword, DM with button |
| Maker School (Nick) | "$184 now, $194 soon" on the page | n/a | price steps tied to member counts |
| Member plan | $77 for the first 100 members, then $97 and slowly up | n/a | member-post question |
| Member plan | guitar program at $40 for a limited time, then $50 | n/a | asked how long the window should be |
- Josh's twist: announce the increase ahead of time and change the annual price too; many members buy right before it.
- Another member reported $18K upfront cash and $3K MRR, mostly from an annual plan, with a week left on the increase deadline, and was pushing toward $30K+.

### Framework: Annual Plan Band
**When to use:** Setting an annual option, or deciding whether to push annual in a promotion.
**Steps/Template:** The lesson advises an annual price 20-40% below 12 x monthly.
| Monthly | 12 x monthly | Annual at 40% off | Annual at 20% off |
|---|---|---|---|
| $25 | $300 | $180 | $240 |
| $50 | $600 | $360 | $480 |
| $97-100 | $1,164-1,200 | ~$700-720 | ~$930-960 |
| $184 | $2,208 | $1,325 | $1,766 |
- Observed deviations: Maker School $184/month vs $1,104/year (six months of price, a 50% discount); Samuel G's $25/month vs $200/year (a 33% discount, in band); James Lu prices annual at slightly under four months of monthly because SAT prep usually runs ~3 months and the gap to the next test is 4 months, which smooths a seasonal cash dip (a much bigger discount than the band, justified by the cycle).
- Treat annual as cash up front but remember leaderboard MRR counts it divided by 12.
- Nick also noticed other communities upselling annual from the sidebar (the caption suggests a "save" label).
- Field update 2026-10-09: Hormozi's traffic post puts the sweet spot for the highest performing groups at about 40% off for annual ("the larger the discount the better") [community/updates/2026-10-09-tips-to-get-more-traffic-from-skool-ads.md].

### Framework: Price-Descent Anchoring and Reframes
**When to use:** Making a modest price feel obviously fair, especially to a cash-strapped audience.
**Steps/Template:**
1. Anchor high with what the market charges for the same result.
2. Descend: show you could have charged that but did not.
3. Land on a number and shrink the unit (per day, per coffee).
4. Explain what the price is for: accountability, not profit.
- Samuel G's live: he recalls a $12,500 three-day seminar and a further $30,000 for hand-holding, says mentors told him to charge $15,000, refuses $10,000, then $1,000, and finally offers $47, then a launch special at $25/month or $200/year for 15 spots, "less than a dollar a day". In the Q&A he also reduces it to "83 cents" a day and explains $25 over $49 or $97 this way: people are broke and it should be silly to refuse, yet still non-zero to keep members accountable.
- Oscar Patel: $39 is "illogical" to refuse if it saves hours of searching; compares price with time value.
- Nick Saraev: frame as business investment (a $100 outlay versus a possible five-figure return), with a "what's inside is worth $X" stack he admits is informal.

### Framework: Audience Affordability and Cost-to-Serve
**When to use:** The price feels either hard to justify to your audience or risky for your margins.
**Steps/Template:**

### Framework: Tiers and High-Ticket Add-ons
**When to use:** Considering a second price point.
**Steps/Template:**
- Jade's three tiers ($50 standard, $100, $200 per month) replace the old yearly pricing; the $50 tier lets someone enter, look around, then upgrade, which she prefers to free trials.
- Member 452e (230 members): at a weekly mastermind he seeded a high-ticket slot; 5 of ~30 attendees took a $500/month tier, moving MRR from $8,478 to $10,738 in about an hour (member count slipped from 230 to 228, so net growth ~$2,260, which the post reports as +$2,401).
- On the onboarding call the host calls a $50 base offer plus a roughly $1,000/month VIP tier a common strategy, and notes high-ticket recurring revenue counts toward the leaderboard.
- Nick Saraev's discipline: he calculates a $50/month side product sold to 20% of ~2,700 members as ~$27K/month, about 9% of gross, and decides pressing the main button is a better use of time.
- Samuel G adds no upsells or higher tiers by choice.
- James Lu tried a high-ticket program with a team, found it did not convert and cost training time, and returned to a self-paced low-ticket model.
- Member structures asked about: free tier, $20 premium and $250 VIP; Free, $47/month Pro and $1,250/month done-with-you Program (worry: Pro reveals all the IP); $67-97/month low-ticket community feeding a 33-person pay-in-full group. No captured answers; the course's access-over-content clips suggest the tiers should differ by access, support and implementation rather than by hiding information (my inference).

### Framework: One-Time Course to Recurring Membership
**When to use:** You currently sell a one-time course or kit and want recurring revenue.
**Steps/Template:**
- Josh Madakor: a course sold once, while members may want support for years; the business incentive and economics favour a subscription. He worried members would consume a course in a month and leave, but his average lifetime value is above $800, because members stay for community and attention, not only classroom content.
- Keep the outcome constant and change the container (his coaching, then course, then community all delivered job outcomes).
- Roderick Casilli's win post: moving from mid-ticket lifetime memberships to a low-ticket Skool reached ~$17K MRR within a month.
- Member scenarios without captured answers: EUR 509 one-time vs $49-99/month with 20-25 hours of content; a 329-pound lash training kit with certificates where the member does not want people to pay one month and leave.
- Practical levers shown elsewhere in the course: ongoing weekly recordings, daily accountability tasks, community, personal replies.

**When to use:** After the first month, when cancellations arrive.

### Framework: Migrating Existing Payers
**When to use:** Moving subscribers from Kajabi, Circle, Patreon, Stripe or similar.
**Steps/Template:**
1. Sell it as an upgrade, not a platform change.
2. Show conviction and add an exclusive bonus only in Skool.
3. Give existing subscribers a founder rate for about a week before the public launch at the higher price.
4. Set a firm end date for the old community and remind them several times.
5. Smooth payment: ask them to join on Skool and refund their latest payment on the old platform.
6. For holdouts, keep billing on the old tool but invite them free.
- Open member questions: whether to move 1:1 coaching clients off Stripe, and whether to offer different tiers for coaching versus community.

## Key Principles
- Pick a band, then a point in it; do not spend days on the exact digit (Nick: 46 versus 44 versus 48 was arbitrary).
- Starting a little low and rising with value is common among mentors, but starting too low can attract price-sensitive buyers and cannot be reversed gracefully.
- Keep price changes rare, announced and tied to new value.
- Member-count triggers can create momentum; date triggers create a rush then a drop.
- Subscription value compounds when members stay; protect the stay with activity, clarity and visible price.
- Fewer members at a higher price and many members at a low price both reached $100k; the best fit is the one your traffic and audience support.
- A price you can explain per day or per hour sells itself to hesitant buyers.
- A small payment can be a feature: it makes people show up.
- Annual plans help cash and lock-in but leaderboard MRR counts them divided by 12.
- Existing members keep their rate, so each raise builds a tiered base; plan for old cheaper members.
- Run the numbers on side products; a $50/month add-on to a fifth of members may be less than 10% of gross.
- Calculate cost to serve before pricing anything that uses compute, tools or coaching time.
- Use Pro when MRR passes about $1,300; below, Hobby's lower fixed fee wins.

## Decision Tools
- IF you have never sold before THEN price at the middle of your niche band and plan to adjust.
- IF past offers sold at the high end THEN start in the upper third of the band.
- IF your audience is short on cash or a global mix THEN consider a lower entry price or a lower tier rather than a free trial.
- IF you plan to raise price weekly or monthly THEN tie each raise to something new (calls, modules, features) and cap how often you do it.
- IF your MRR is below about $1,300 THEN the Hobby plan costs less; above it THEN Pro is cheaper (and Pro includes affiliates and custom URL).
- IF you want annual THEN price it at 60-80% of 12 x monthly (20-40% off), unless the product has a seasonal cycle that justifies a deeper cut.
- IF churn is high with "no time" reasons THEN revisit onboarding, pacing and clear outcome steps before changing the price.
- IF members leave unhappy with results and your policy is generous THEN refund promptly; Nick and James both describe doing so and say it tends to pay back in goodwill and later wins.
- IF you want a quick MRR jump from existing members THEN test a limited high-ticket slot after delivering value.
- IF the founding deadline passes THEN raise the price as promised; extending it teaches buyers to wait.
- IF unit costs rise with usage THEN set a usage cap or price above a heavy user's cost.
- IF free users aren't opening the classroom THEN use paid-only or a low paid entry tier instead.

## Anti-Patterns
- Spending weeks on the "perfect" price; the mentors treat it as a quick test.
- Changing price repeatedly without added value (Josh regrets instability; Oscar warns about goodwill).
- Starting extremely low then needing to push early members to pay more or leave (Nick's first community).
- Raising so high that part of your audience can no longer join and you cannot lower it (Nick on $184).
- Announcing a "last chance" with no real deadline or spot limit.
- Treating one-time payments as leaderboard MRR.
- Choosing a plan tier by habit rather than by the $1,300 MRR break-even.
- Letting a free tier fill with passive members who never see value (Jade's trial results, Samuel's reversal).
- Pricing a compute-heavy lab without knowing cost per active member (Josh's start at $384).
- Chasing a side product that generates less than a tenth of the main stream (Nick's math).
- Forgetting seasonality: a product tied to exam dates can lose most members in one month (James's December).
- Putting a high-ticket tier next to a low tier without distinguishing access, support or implementation (member worry about "all the IP for $47").
