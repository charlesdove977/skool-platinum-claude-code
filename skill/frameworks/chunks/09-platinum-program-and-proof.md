## Core Concept
Skool Platinum is a selective, time-boxed competition for educational creators with roughly 100K followers who want to reach $100k per month on Skool. It is built from a study of the communities already at that level, and the course, mentor calls and ranking board are all reverse-engineered from them. The "big lesson" is that those owners went all-in: Skool became their number-one focus, their only link in bio, a regular topic in their content, and the only offer they sold. The proof in the program is extensive (eleven documented $100k/month communities, mentor revenue numbers, and cohort-1 results where many groups went from zero to $17K-$52K MRR growth in about a month), and it points to a consistent conclusion: audience size matters less than launching fast, sending traffic to one place, and showing up every day.

### Framework: Growth Boost Mechanics for Program Members
**When to use:** Setting the group up so Skool's ads and search surfaces work in your favor.
**Steps/Template:**
- **Cost:** a 30% commission only on members Skool brings through ads or search. Members you bring yourself pay only the standard transaction fee (about 2.9% on Pro). Bidding more raises traffic and lowers margin.
- **First ad:** your cover image (shown after the first handful of members). So the cover must communicate the value proposition, show your face, and convey the theme.
- **Second stage:** if the cover ad performs, Skool may run your organic content as ads.
- **Focus:** the call advises focusing on sending traffic, converting it, and keeping members happy rather than chasing the ideal ad copy; groups that provide a good customer experience get picked up more.
- Example outcomes: one member had more members from Growth Boost than from a 100K-subscriber YouTube channel; another credited about $10K MRR in a week; a Taekwondo group featured in ads got 92 signups from 5,208 visitors (1.8%).

### Framework: The Big Lesson (All-In)
**When to use:** Deciding whether to take on a second offer, an extra link, or a new project.
**Steps/Template:**
- Definition borrowed from Hormozi: commitment is "the elimination of alternatives." All-in = eliminate all alternatives.
- Four behaviors shared by every $100k/mo educator the team studied:
  1. Made promoting their Skool the number-one focus.
  2. Used the Skool as the only link in bio on all platforms.
  3. Regularly promoted it in content.
  4. Did not get distracted selling multiple offers.
- The call's example: Oscar Patel ignored constant pitches ("promote this", "let me do your funnel") and stayed with Skool.
- Nuance from the call: one link in bio is a best practice, not a requirement. Members with brand deals or other obligations are not removed for having multiple links; they simply lower their odds.

### Framework: Launch First, Build Later (Nick's Launch Playbook)
**When to use:** Standing up a minimum viable group quickly; this is the recommended launch strategy in the lesson.
**Steps/Template:**
1. Create a blank community and pay the Skool fee; the payment pre-commits you.
2. Spend about 20 minutes on simple assets (white-on-black text, minimalist logo).
3. Record only the first one or two days of the program (about 2 hours total); build the rest from member questions. Nick's first program was "14-Day Agency."
4. Message about 20 top followers and warm audience members with a personal ask: join, post an intro, and give your honest view.
5. Seed the community so the first organic visitor sees active, successful people.
6. Make the group public.
7. Start at a low price (his first was $20/month), add a weekly community call, then raise price gradually as recorded content accumulates.
8. Habit framing: aim for something you can do "80% right every day for a year," rather than 100% right today.
- Nick later regrets pricing too low: it attracted unserious members; in hindsight he would start at roughly $50 to $60 a month (the transcript's figure for his original price is garbled, around $20).
- The lesson's justification: creators usually guess their audience wants a 10-part course and guess wrong; building live with members avoids that.

### Framework: What the Examples Share
**When to use:** Auditing a plan against the patterns, without relying on any single mentor.
**Steps/Template:**
1. One destination: the Skool is the only link, and content points at it.
2. Fast launch with a minimal classroom; the product is built with members.
3. A visible owner presence (daily posting, live sessions, access to the creator).
4. Proof on display (wins categories, testimonial videos, screenshots).
5. A specific outcome niche (for example Nick's first paying customer in 90 days, Oscar's health-equals-looks).
6. Price choices vary widely ($25 to $226), so price follows the audience and offer, not a universal number.
7. Traffic strategy differs (YouTube evergreen, Instagram DMs, TikTok Live), but each owner has a primary channel.
8. Persistence: nearly every story contains a stretch of low results before growth.

## Key Principles
- Treat the program as a race against a published calendar; launching early gives a head start on both the leaderboard and the first checkpoint.
- Rank is about net recurring growth, not audience: annual plans count at one-twelfth, one-time sales do not, and churn subtracts.
- The cohort evidence says a launched, imperfect group beats a polished unlaunched one.
- Skool's ads and search surfaces behave as a tailwind: they amplify a group that already converts and retains.
- Focus is the differentiator the team sees across the $100k owners; best practices raise odds but are not entry requirements.
- Fewer, more committed members at a higher price can reach the same revenue as many members at a low price (604 members at $226 vs. 7.9k at $39).
- Copy the observable parts: click leaders' groups and profiles and see how they promote and convert.
- Frequency of presence beats elaborate community structure (Nick calls his own structure among his weakest points).

## Decision Tools
- IF you have not yet created the Skool THEN do that first; the later steps and the leaderboard depend on a live group.
- IF your current MRR is under ~$1,300 THEN the numbers favor the Hobby plan; above it, Pro leaves you with more after fees (and Pro includes affiliates and custom URL).
- IF the date is before November 1 THEN launching now lets October growth count toward the first $1K target (rankings count from Oct 1).
- IF a member sells a one-time product THEN it will not add to the leaderboard, so add a recurring tier if the goal is rank.
- IF you sell annual plans THEN divide by 12 to estimate leaderboard impact.
- IF you cannot show a pattern for how leaders convert THEN click their about page and social profile from the rankings tab.
- IF you have brand deals that demand a second link THEN keep them; the program treats the single link as a best practice and does not remove members for it.
- IF you are delaying launch to perfect the classroom THEN compare with Member f1e9 Bult (a 1:20 video, no course) and Nick Saraev (first two days recorded).
- IF your audience is smaller than most listed examples THEN note that Samuel G topped the cohort board with 52K TikTok followers and that audience size showed no clear link to speed.
- IF you are Pro-priced low and attracting unserious members THEN consider Nick's lesson: he would start around $50 to $60 rather than $20.
- IF you cannot reply to everything yourself THEN do the per-member time math before hiring (his rate was ~0.02 minutes per member per day).
- IF new members ask for access to everything THEN Nick's rule: verify they have already progressed; otherwise point them back to action and offer a refund if they object.

## Anti-Patterns
- Waiting until the timer is nearly out; the host says members often discover late that it is harder than expected.
- Spending weeks preparing a classroom or copy before any member has validated it.
- Pursuing a second offer, a software idea or a partner funnel instead of improving the Skool (Nick's math shows a new product would add under 9% to gross).
- Counting on one-time sales to climb the leaderboard; they do not register.
- Not filling in social links in profile settings, which limits Skool's ability to advertise the group.
- Expecting ads on an empty community; Skool's ads start after launch and a few hundred page clicks.
- Treating audience size as a requirement; the board is platform agnostic and ranked by growth.
- Setting the first price so low that it attracts members who dispute every dollar.
- Treating program milestones as impossible from afar instead of focusing on the next checkpoint.
- Passing community management to a low-level manager when the community is built around the owner's own voice (Nick's view).
